One day of light. A lifetime of exploration.

NASA announces Voyager 1 reaching one light-day from Earth on 18 November 2026 at 02:16:07 PST / 10:16:07 UTC, at approximately 25.902 billion km. This is a distance milestone, not a change in spacecraft speed. A reply to a command would need about 48 hours for the round trip, before processing time.

How to play

Fly 50 yearly levels, from 1977 to 2026. Quiet years last 45 seconds; eight milestone years last 80–100 seconds. Unboosted flight time is 43 minutes 30 seconds, or about 47 minutes including automatic transitions. A completion screen appears for four seconds, then the next year begins automatically. Your journey score carries forward; each year starts with fresh pickups, a full warp reserve and a ready pulse. Pause, About and Look around also stop the transition countdown. The year selector starts a new journey from any year; Replay year resets the journey score.

Steer with arrows / WASD or drag on touch screens. Gold records score 100, moving UFOs 750; chain pickups up to 5×. UFOs refill warp. Hold Space or Warp to accelerate. Press E or Pickup pulse for 1.8 seconds of wider collection reach, with a 14-second cooldown. P pauses. Best scores are saved per year, separately from the earlier four-chapter version.

The cyan Pickup center ring follows the golden body beneath Voyager’s dish: aim with that point, not the dish edge or antenna tips. The faint outer halo shows gold collection reach in the flight cross-section as you pass a pickup, not a long-distance magnet. Gold and UFO pickup radii are 25% wider than before; UFOs still have slightly more forgiving reach than gold and can be collected just outside the halo. Exact surface contact is not required. During a pickup pulse the marker and halo turn violet, and the halo expands to the pulse’s unchanged reach.

Sound & music

Audio is off until you choose Sound on. Mix has separate Music and Effects controls. A quiet original space-music loop accompanies flight; record chimes rise with your combo, UFOs have a distinct reward, and pulse and warp have their own effects. Planetary encounters and the light-day approach add a musical accent; each year ends with a short flourish, with a fuller finale in 2026. Pause, About, Mix, Look around and leaving the page silence playback until you resume. All sounds are synthesized locally, with no audio downloads. Audio choices last for this page session.

The route is real

Loading data provenance…

All 50 levels use Voyager 1—not Voyager 2. Each level follows its calendar year; 1977 begins on 6 September, the first available bundled JPL state after the 5 September launch. Subsequent years meet at 1 January with no time gaps. The 2026 finale ends exactly at NASA’s announced November milestone time. Milestone years highlight launch, Jupiter (1979), Saturn (1980), the Pale Blue Dot (1990), overtaking Pioneer 10 (1998), the termination shock (2004), the heliopause (2012), and one light-day (2026). The early Voyager 1 JPL solution is an approximate mission-design reconstruction, not perfect historical navigation telemetry.

Coordinates are heliocentric ecliptic J2000; time is UTC. Cubic Hermite interpolation uses JPL positions and velocities. Planet positions come from the same data bundle. A floating camera origin avoids astronomical precision loss. The Earth light-time readout is 3D geometric range divided by c; it can differ slightly from NASA’s operational milestone estimate. It is not a live spacecraft feed.

Planetary moons

Jupiter’s Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, and Saturn’s Titan and Enceladus use real, hourly NASA/JPL Horizons states relative to their parent planet during the respective encounter. The moon data spans 23 February–15 March 1979 and 2–22 November 1980, not the full calendar year. Moons appear only while that encounter window is being played. Orbital directions evolve with the game’s historical date. Orbit distances are compressed and moon sizes enlarged to make the systems readable beside enlarged planets; these are not true-scale flyby distances. Surfaces are original procedural illustrations, not spacecraft photographs.

The fun is fictional

UFOs, coins, pulse fields, scoring and warp are arcade inventions. Touching a UFO awards more points than gold and restores some warp charge. Voyager has never reported an alien encounter. Sideways steering does not alter the underlying JPL path. Planet sizes, spacecraft scale and time are exaggerated. The occasional rocky meteoroids beside the route are non-colliding illustrative scenery, not tracked objects or a real dense asteroid field. They have no fiery trails: a meteor is the atmospheric light phenomenon, not a burning rock in empty space. Beyond the heliopause, the colored haze and celebratory light-day gate are artistic—not observed nebulae, physical boundaries or real objects near Voyager. Entering interstellar space does not mean leaving the entire solar system or Oort Cloud.

Finding your way around the sky

Vega, Altair, Deneb, Antares, Polaris and the Galactic Center have approximate J2000 equatorial coordinates converted to ecliptic directions. Their markers are distant direction references, not close flybys; proper motion and parallax are not modeled. The brighter Milky Way and layered blue-white and warm starfield are decorative, not a registered star catalog or a view from Voyager’s camera. Stars do not flash or twinkle. Use Look around and drag to explore. Disable Sky names to hide labels without dimming the stars.
